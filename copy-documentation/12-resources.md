### Article body
**[H2]** The problem with scattered channels
**[Body]** If your team is growing, you've hit the same wall every support team hits: customers message you wherever is easiest for them — WhatsApp at night, Instagram DMs after seeing an ad, the website chat widget mid-checkout. Someone still has to open all three, read every message, and decide who answers what.
**[Body]** A simple "is this still in stock?" ends up in the same queue as a refund dispute. Whoever reads messages first ends up doing the sorting, not the support — and the customer waits either way.

**[H2]** Enter One Inbox
**[Body]** One Inbox is exactly what it sounds like: every conversation, from every channel, lands in a single list — tagged by where it came from, and already marked with what happened to it. The screenshot above is a real inbox view. Nothing is invented for this post.
**[Body]** Notice the last column: **Resolved**, **Booked**, **Lead saved**, **With Priya**. That's not a message count — it's an outcome. The agent already did something with each conversation before a human ever had to look at the list.

**[H2]** Step 1: every channel, one place
**[Body]** Connecting a channel takes minutes, not an integration project — WhatsApp Business API, Instagram DMs and your website's chat widget all route into the same inbox. The WA/IG/Web tags in the list above are the actual source of each message, not a mockup label.

**[Callout H4]** We only publish what we can show
**[Callout body]** Every screenshot in this post is a real inbox and dashboard view, not a mockup drawn to make a point. If a number or status looks unglamorous, that's because it's real.

**[H2]** Step 2: the agent still answers first
**[Body]** One Inbox isn't a better way to read messages faster — it's what's left over after the Support Agent has already answered most of them. Order status, policy questions, "is this in stock" — all of that gets resolved before it ever needs a human glance. What lands in front of your team is mostly what the agent flagged, not everything.

**[H2]** Step 3: handover, when it's actually needed
**[Body]** Meera's refund question above didn't get a scripted answer — it went straight to Priya, tagged **With Priya** so nobody else picks it up by accident and nothing falls through twice. That's the actual design principle: the agent handles what it can resolve safely, and hands off cleanly — with the full conversation history attached — the moment a real judgment call is needed.

**[Inline CTA H3]** Stop reading tickets to find the ones that matter.
**[Inline CTA body]** Connect your channels once. Everything after that is triaged for you.
**[CTA]** Start free trial

**[H2]** What this changes
**[Body]** Nothing about this replaces your support team — it removes the sorting step they were never actually hired to do. The team spends its time on the refund, the escalation, the judgment call. The password reset, the stock check, the "what are your hours" — those never reach a human queue at all.
**[Body]** If you want to see what the underlying automation looks like once a conversation needs to run on a schedule rather than answer instantly, that's the workflow engine — covered in the next post.

### Article body
**[H2]** The problem with manual follow-up
**[Body]** Someone adds a product to their cart, gets distracted, and leaves. If a human is meant to notice and follow up, that follow-up happens whenever someone next checks the dashboard — an hour later, the next morning, or not at all. By then the moment that made them want it has usually passed.
**[Body]** A blanket "10% off everything" email blast recovers some of that, but it trains customers to wait for a discount instead of buying when they meant to.

**[H2]** The workflow, node by node
**[Body]** The diagram above is a real, live example from Workflows — not a diagram drawn for this post. It's a five-node chain: a trigger, a wait, a check, and two actions. Nothing about it needs a developer to set up.

**[H2]** Step 1: the trigger and the wait
**[Body]** The workflow starts the moment a cart is abandoned — in this example, a ₹2,400 cart. It doesn't message immediately. It waits 30 minutes first, because a message that arrives while someone's still mid-checkout reads as pushy, not helpful.

**[Callout H4]** The wait is configurable, not fixed
**[Callout body]** 30 minutes is what's set in this example. Some stores wait 15 minutes, others wait a few hours — it depends on how considered the purchase is.

**[H2]** Step 2: the check that matters
**[Body]** Before sending anything, the workflow checks one thing: did the order actually get placed in the meantime? If it did, the workflow just stops — nobody gets a "did you forget something?" message for a purchase they already completed. That single connector node is what separates a workflow from a dumb timed blast.

**[H2]** Step 3: the message, then the handoff
**[Body]** Only if the cart is still genuinely abandoned does the WhatsApp message go out. And it isn't a one-way blast — the same agent that sent it stays in the conversation, answers whatever comes back ("is this still in stock?", "can I get it in a different size?"), and shares the checkout link directly in the chat.

**[Inline CTA H3]** Stop losing carts to a discount email nobody opens.
**[Inline CTA body]** This exact workflow — trigger, wait, check, message — is ready to turn on.
**[CTA]** Start free trial

**[H2]** Why this works better than a discount blast
**[Body]** A scheduled email blast treats every abandoned cart the same. This workflow treats each one as a conversation that's still open — it waits for the right moment, checks whether it's even still relevant, and then picks the conversation back up rather than starting a new one. That's the difference between "recovering revenue" and "training customers to expect a discount."
**[Body]** The same trigger→wait→check→action shape runs the other four workflows in the platform too — following up on a quiet lead, reminding a customer of an appointment, or sending a broadcast. If you want to see how one inbox handles everything these workflows generate, that's the previous post.

### Article body
**[H2]** The problem with after-hours leads
**[Body]** Real estate enquiries don't arrive on business hours. Someone scrolling listings at 11pm is a genuinely warm lead — but if the reply comes the next morning, they've usually already messaged two other agencies by then. Speed is most of the qualification.

**[H2]** The conversation, start to finish
**[Body]** The screenshot above is the shape of a real enquiry, not a script written for this post — the timestamps run 11:02 PM to 11:05 PM, start to finish. Three minutes, one conversation, one booked site visit.

**[H2]** Step 1: qualify budget first
**[Body]** Before showing anything, the agent asks the one question that actually narrows the search — budget. "Around 65 lakhs" is enough to filter out listings that would waste everyone's time on a site visit neither side wants.

**[Callout H4]** It's working from your real inventory
**[Callout body]** Prestige Meridian and its ₹64L price aren't invented for this post — the agent answers from whatever listings and pricing you've actually connected.

**[H2]** Step 2: show one listing, not ten
**[Body]** Instead of a wall of options, the agent shares a single well-matched listing as a card — distance from the tech park, price, a link — inline in the same chat. One good match moves the conversation forward faster than ten average ones.

**[H2]** Step 3: book the site visit directly
**[Body]** The moment interest is confirmed, the agent offers real open slots — not "someone will call you to schedule" — and locks in the one the lead picks. By 11:05 PM, there's a confirmed Saturday 4pm site visit, and a promise (that the agent will keep) to send the exact location and the human agent's number ahead of time.

**[Inline CTA H3]** Stop losing after-hours leads to whoever replies first.
**[Inline CTA body]** Qualify budget, share the right listing, and book the visit — automatically.

**[H2]** What your sales team gets instead
**[Body]** Nobody on your team had to be awake at 11pm for this to happen — but the moment they're back online, they see a qualified lead with a confirmed site visit already on the calendar, not a cold enquiry to chase. That's the actual shift: the agent doesn't replace the site visit or the close, it makes sure a real one gets booked instead of going cold overnight.
**[Body]** Once a lead is this qualified, seeing how it shows up across every conversation your team has — not just this one — is what One Inbox is built for.

### Article body
**[H2]** The problem with vanity metrics
**[Body]** "1,000 messages handled this month" sounds impressive and tells you almost nothing. Was it 1,000 easy questions, or 1,000 people the agent quietly failed and nobody noticed? A message count can't answer that. An outcome can.

**[H2]** The numbers, from a real account
**[Body]** The dashboard above is a real account's numbers, not an illustration built to look good. 412 conversations. 358 answered solo. 24 meetings booked. 30 passed to a human. Every number on this page is from a real account or clearly marked as an example — we don't publish numbers we can't show you.

**[H2]** What it handled alone
**[Body]** 358 out of 412 conversations closed without a human touching them — not "the agent replied," but the conversation actually reached a resolution. That's the number that answers "is this actually working," not the raw message count.

**[Callout H4]** The dashboard updates in real time
**[Callout body]** A plain-English weekly summary lands in your inbox too, so you don't have to go looking for these numbers to know how the week went.

**[H2]** What it's worth
**[Body]** 24 meetings booked isn't a vanity stat — each one is a calendar slot that got filled without anyone on your team touching a scheduling link. That's the difference between "the agent was busy" and "the agent produced something you'd have paid a person to produce."

**[H2]** Where it's weak
**[Body]** 30 conversations got passed to a human — and that number matters just as much as the other three. Every one of those is a question the agent couldn't answer safely, which points straight at the page or document that needs fixing, not at a vague "AI isn't perfect" shrug. A dashboard that only shows wins is hiding the most useful part of the report.

**[H2]** Why we publish it this way
**[Body]** A summary, not a spreadsheet: how many conversations closed without you, what it's worth in bookings and updates, and where it's still weak — those are the only three things worth checking a dashboard for. Everything else is noise dressed up as a metric.
**[Body]** Those 24 booked meetings and 30 handovers are all outcomes generated by conversations that started somewhere specific — a workflow, a channel, an inbox. If you haven't seen how those upstream pieces work yet, One Inbox and Workflows are where they're covered.
